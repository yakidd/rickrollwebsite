I went from fixing hospital printers to building an AI that hunts hackers. What it caught at 3am made me shut my laptop 😳

Nobody tells you this about cybersecurity. A decade in healthcare IT, one career jump, and a 2-minute video I probably shouldn't be sharing.

People ask how someone goes from fixing printers in an emergency department to running engineering for a security operations centre. The honest answer is that I never really changed jobs. I just kept following the same problem into bigger rooms.

It started long before anyone paid me. From about 2004 I was the family's IT department: building PCs, overclocking things that probably shouldn't have been overclocked, and rescuing laptops. Some of you reading this were my first customers. I'm still on call, apparently.

The thread that runs through all of it

Healthcare IT is a lesson in translation. Every scanner, every lab system, every patient record speaks its own dialect, and HL7 messages are how they shout at each other. My job for years was catching those messages, making sense of them, and making sure the right thing happened next. Miss one and a scan doesn't reach a radiologist.

Security turned out to be exactly the same problem with higher stakes. Firewalls, endpoints, identity platforms and cloud logs all speak different languages too. The SOC's job is to catch those signals, make sense of them, and act before the damage is done.

Normalise everything. Get the data right first and every clever thing you build on top gets easier.

That was true for patient data and it's true for security telemetry. It's also, it turns out, the bit that makes AI genuinely useful rather than a gimmick.

Where AI comes in

A SOC analyst's day is mostly triage: reading an alert, pulling context from five different tools, deciding whether it matters. It's skilled work, but a lot of it is repetitive. I've spent enough nights on call to know how it feels at 3am.

So we're building a platform where every signal becomes a clean, consistent event, and an AI analyst works alongside the humans. It gathers the context, explains what it found and drafts a recommendation. The person stays firmly in charge of the decision.
